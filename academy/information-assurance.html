Information Assurance

Manage assurance as an evidence-backed, AO-level continuous process. This course teaches how to use Assessment Objectives, the Evidence Fabric, and the Assurance Calendar to operate a governance program that can stand up to AO-level review and external audit at any point in the operating cycle.

~3 hours 6 modules + knowledge checks Practitioner Certification eligible
Before you begin: This course requires access to Governance & Assurance in CloudSignals, including the Evidence Fabric and Assessment Objectives views. If these aren't visible in your tenant, confirm your role has GRC access with your platform administrator.
1

Assurance Fundamentals

The difference between implementing a control and demonstrating it works.

Implementation vs. effectiveness

The most important distinction in information assurance is between implementation and effectiveness. Implementation means a control exists — a policy is written, a tool is deployed, a process is documented. Effectiveness means the control produces the expected risk reduction in practice — access is actually restricted as the policy describes, the tool actually detects what it claims to detect, the process is actually followed.

Most governance programs verify implementation. Few continuously verify effectiveness. The consequence is that assurance evidence — the "proof" that controls work — is based on what was true at one point in time, not what is true now. CloudSignals addresses this by treating assurance as a continuous process anchored to Assessment Objectives, not a periodic audit event anchored to report dates.

Confidence, residual risk, and the AO boundary

Control confidence is a composite score reflecting how recent the evidence is, how many of the control's Assessment Objectives have been satisfied, and whether the evidence came from automated detection or human attestation. Residual risk is the risk that remains after all controls are applied — it is a function of both the threat and the confidence that your controls are actually effective against it. The Authorizing Official (AO) is the named individual accountable for accepting residual risk on behalf of the organization. In CloudSignals, AO-level assurance means the residual risk picture is accurate enough for a named authority to make a defensible decision.

Identify your AO: Confirm who in your organization holds the Authorizing Official role — the person accountable for accepting residual risk. This person should be named in the platform as the governance decision-maker.
Distinguish implementation from effectiveness in your current controls: For three controls in your UCB, note whether you have evidence of implementation only, or evidence of ongoing effectiveness.
Review your current confidence score distribution: Navigate to Governance & Assurance and note how many controls have high, medium, and low confidence scores.
Knowledge Check
What is the difference between control implementation and control effectiveness?
Implementation is the technical configuration; effectiveness is the policy documentation that describes that configuration.
Implementation confirms a control exists; effectiveness confirms it produces the expected risk reduction in practice — they can diverge when controls are present but not functioning as designed.
Implementation is verified by automated scanners; effectiveness is verified only by external auditors.
They are equivalent terms — a control that is implemented is by definition effective.
Move on when you've identified your AO and reviewed the implementation vs. effectiveness gap in your controls.

Assessment Objectives

The granular, testable assertions that make assurance verifiable rather than assumed.

What an Assessment Objective is

An Assessment Objective (AO) is a specific, testable assertion about a control. Where a control says "Encrypt all data at rest," an Assessment Objective says "Encryption at rest is enabled on all production database instances using AES-256 or stronger." AOs are first-class objects in CloudSignals — they have their own status, evidence requirements, and reviewer assignments separate from the control itself.

This matters because a single control may have multiple AOs, each requiring different evidence. "Encrypt all data at rest" may decompose into: (1) encryption enabled on all database instances, (2) encryption enabled on all storage buckets, (3) encryption keys are managed by customer-managed KMS, (4) key rotation is configured. Each of these can be satisfied independently, and partial satisfaction is trackable — you can see exactly which sub-requirement is unmet.

In the platform — Review an Assessment Objective and map evidence
1
Navigate to Governance & Assurance > Assessment Objectives. Review the AO list — note status distribution (Satisfied, Unsatisfied, Partially Satisfied, Not Assessed).
2
Filter for "Unsatisfied" AOs. Click into the highest-priority one. Review: the specific assertion being made, the evidence type required, and the linked UCB control.
3
Review the evidence currently linked to this AO. If evidence exists but the AO is still "Unsatisfied," check evidence freshness — stale evidence doesn't satisfy an AO even if the artifact exists.
4
Assign a reviewer to the AO if one isn't set. The reviewer is responsible for verifying evidence quality before the AO is marked as Satisfied.
5
Navigate to a "Satisfied" AO and review the evidence chain — trace from the AO through each evidence artifact to its source and reviewer. This is the audit trail an assessor would inspect.
Review unsatisfied AOs: List your top 5 unsatisfied AOs by linked control criticality. These represent your highest-priority assurance gaps.
Assign reviewers to all active AOs: Confirm every AO has a named reviewer assigned. Unreviewed AOs cannot transition to Satisfied status regardless of evidence quality.
Trace a satisfied AO to its evidence chain: For one Satisfied AO, verify the evidence lineage is complete — each artifact has a source, freshness indicator, and reviewer attestation.
Knowledge Check
Why are Assessment Objectives first-class objects in CloudSignals rather than just sub-items of controls?
AOs are required by FedRAMP and NIST 800-53 to be tracked separately from controls for federal authorization.
AOs are the granular, testable assertions that auditors actually evaluate — having them as first-class objects enables precise evidence mapping, partial satisfaction tracking, and reviewer accountability at the assertion level, not just the control level.
AOs are automatically generated by the scanner and don't require human review, making them faster to process than control-level evidence.
AOs replace controls entirely in the UCB for organizations seeking ISO 27001 certification.
Move on when you've reviewed unsatisfied AOs and confirmed reviewers are assigned.

Evidence Fabric

The lineage chain that connects raw artifacts to governance-grade assurance.

Evidence requirements and artifact types

The Evidence Fabric is the system that connects evidence artifacts to Assessment Objectives, tracks their lineage, and evaluates their quality. Not all evidence is equal. Evidence types are ranked by the confidence they contribute: Automated evidence (scanner results, configuration exports, log samples) has consistent provenance but no human interpretation. Attested evidence (reviewer attestations, signed confirmations) provides human accountability but depends on the reviewer's knowledge. Observed evidence (direct inspection, witnessed demonstrations) is the highest trust tier but requires assessor time.

For most UCB controls, the expected evidence mix is: automated scanner data for implementation verification, plus human attestation for effectiveness verification. A control "satisfied" by scanner data alone meets a lower confidence threshold than one supported by scanner data and attestation.

In the platform — Map evidence to a control and review lineage
1
Navigate to Governance & Assurance > Evidence Fabric. Sort by "Freshness" to see which evidence artifacts are oldest. These are your staleness risks.
2
Click into a stale artifact. Review: its source (automated vs. manual), its linked AO, the reviewer who last attested to it, and the date of last attestation.
3
Click Refresh Evidence or Re-attest. If the underlying configuration or log can be re-collected automatically, trigger a re-collection. If it requires manual attestation, assign to the control owner with a deadline.
4
For a fully fresh AO, trace the complete evidence lineage: AO → evidence artifact → source system → collection method → reviewer attestation. This complete chain is what a NIST, ISO, or SOC assessor would inspect.
Identify stale evidence artifacts: List all evidence artifacts older than your assessment cycle (typically 90 days for most controls, 30 days for critical). Create refresh tasks for each.
Map evidence types to your controls: For your top 10 controls, confirm each has both automated evidence and human attestation — not just one or the other.
Trace one complete evidence lineage: For a Satisfied AO, document the full lineage chain as you would present it to an external auditor.
Knowledge Check
What does "evidence lineage" mean in the context of the Evidence Fabric?
The chronological order in which evidence artifacts were created — used to determine which version is most current.
The traceable chain from a raw artifact through its collection method, reviewer attestation, and mapping to a specific Assessment Objective — providing auditable provenance for every piece of evidence.
The list of all reviewers who have ever attested to a particular control's evidence artifacts.
A reference to the parent compliance framework from which an evidence requirement was derived.
Move on when you've identified stale evidence and traced a complete lineage chain for at least one AO.

Assurance Calendar

Distributing review workload so assurance stays current without sprint-end scrambles.

Why a calendar-driven approach beats event-driven assurance

Event-driven assurance — collecting evidence when an audit is announced — creates predictable problems: evidence is rushed, reviewers are unavailable, and the resulting assurance picture reflects a snapshot taken under pressure. Calendar-driven assurance distributes review work continuously throughout the year, maintaining a rolling state of readiness that can withstand audit scrutiny at any time.

The Assurance Calendar in CloudSignals shows which controls have review windows coming up in the next 7, 30, and 90 days. Controls are scheduled based on their UCB-defined cadence (monthly, quarterly, annual) and the last date evidence was collected and attested. The calendar also surfaces workload distribution — if too many high-effort reviews land in the same week, the calendar helps you redistribute them proactively.

In the platform — Use the Assurance Calendar to plan review workload
1
Navigate to Governance & Assurance > Assurance Calendar. Review the next 30 days. How many controls have review windows? How many reviewers are assigned to upcoming reviews?
2
Identify any weeks with unusually high review workload. For those weeks, consider shifting non-urgent reviews by adjusting the last-collection date or reassigning reviewers to balance capacity.
3
Click into a control due for review within the next 7 days. Confirm: the reviewer is notified, the evidence collection method is documented, and any automated collection is scheduled to run before the review date.
4
For controls with annual review cadence, confirm they haven't been missed. Controls with no review activity in over 12 months are in assurance drift territory — flag for immediate attention.
Review the 30-day assurance calendar: Identify the controls due for review and confirm each has an assigned reviewer and evidence collection method.
Redistribute overloaded review weeks: If any week has more than your team's review capacity, move non-urgent reviews earlier or later to balance workload.
Flag missed annual reviews: Identify any controls with no review activity in 12+ months and create urgent collection tasks for each.
Knowledge Check
What happens in CloudSignals when a scheduled assurance review date is missed?
The control is automatically marked as "Failed" and generates a Critical finding in the findings queue.
The control's evidence is marked stale and its confidence score degrades — this propagates as assurance drift in any assurance report generated for that period.
The review is rescheduled automatically to the next available slot with no impact on confidence scoring.
The control owner receives a notification, but the assurance posture is unaffected until an assessor manually flags the missed review.
Move on when you've reviewed the 30-day calendar and addressed any workload imbalances or missed reviews.

Exceptions & Compensating Controls

How to formally govern residual risk that cannot be immediately remediated.

What a compensating control is

A compensating control is an alternative security measure that reduces the risk associated with a control gap when the primary control cannot be implemented. For example, if a legacy system cannot support multi-factor authentication (the primary control), a compensating control might be: restricting access to that system to a single named IP range, requiring step-up authentication from a bastion host, and implementing session recording. Together, these reduce the risk that MFA was designed to address, even though MFA itself is absent.

Compensating controls require documentation that is more rigorous than a standard exception. You must demonstrate that the compensating measures are proportional to the risk of the missing primary control, that they are actively monitored, and that remediation of the underlying gap is being pursued where feasible.

In the platform — Document an exception with a compensating control
1
Navigate to Projects > Exceptions. Review existing exceptions — note their age, expiry dates, whether a compensating control is documented, and their risk tier.
2
Click Create Exception. Link it to the failing control or finding. Set the exception type (Full Exception vs. Compensating Control).
3
For a Compensating Control exception, fill in: Compensating measures (what you're doing instead), Risk residual (how much risk remains after compensating measures), Monitoring commitment (how you'll detect if the compensating control fails), and Remediation target (when you expect to be able to implement the primary control).
4
Set an expiry date — all exceptions must be time-bounded. Route to the appropriate approver based on your exception authority matrix.
Audit compensating control quality: For each exception with a compensating control, confirm the compensating measures are specific, monitored, and proportional to the risk they're compensating for.
Review exceptions approaching expiry: Exceptions expiring within 30 days need owner review — renew, remediate, or escalate. Do not let exceptions lapse silently.
Identify chronic exceptions: Exceptions renewed more than twice without remediation progress should be escalated to the AO with a remediation plan or explicit permanent acceptance decision.
Knowledge Check
What must a compensating control document include to be accepted as a valid assurance artifact?
The technical specifications of the compensating tool, its vendor name, and its version number.
The specific risk it compensates for, its effectiveness evidence, its applicability scope, how it is monitored, and a defined expiry date for re-evaluation.
CISO approval, a linked security budget item, and a vendor contract confirming the compensating solution is enterprise-licensed.
A comparison to NIST SP 800-53 supplemental guidance confirming the compensating approach is explicitly permitted.
Move on when you've reviewed exception quality and confirmed no exceptions are silently lapsing.

Audit Packages & Readiness Reporting

Packaging your assurance evidence for external assessors and AO-level review.

What an audit package is

An audit package is a point-in-time export of your governance state, formatted for external assessor review. It contains: control states (implemented/effective/not-implemented), Assessment Objective completion status, evidence artifacts with lineage, open exceptions with rationale, and the assurance brief summarizing residual risk. An audit package is what you give an external auditor instead of ad hoc spreadsheets and email chains.

Audit readiness means you can generate an accurate, complete audit package at any time — not just in the week before an assessment. If your assurance calendar is running, evidence is fresh, AOs are reviewed, and exceptions are documented, audit readiness is a continuous state rather than a pre-audit scramble.

In the platform — Generate an audit package
1
Navigate to Reports > Audit Package. Select the scope: which framework (ISO 27001, SOC 2, PCI DSS, or UCB subset), which period, and which managed entities to include.
2
Preview the package before exporting. Review: are there controls marked "Not Assessed"? Are there AOs with stale evidence? These gaps will be visible to an assessor — address them before the audit if possible.
3
Export the package. Review the table of contents: control coverage summary, AO completion rate, exception register, evidence inventory with freshness indicators, and assurance brief.
4
Generate the Executive Assurance Brief separately — this is the one-to-two page narrative that the AO reviews before making risk acceptance decisions. It summarizes the overall posture, open gaps, exceptions, and residual risk without requiring the reader to interpret raw evidence.
Generate your first audit package: Export a package for your primary compliance framework and review its completeness. Note any gaps you would not want an assessor to see.
Produce an executive assurance brief: Generate an assurance brief for your AO and have it reviewed by your governance lead before distribution.
Schedule regular audit readiness checks: Set a quarterly calendar entry to generate a test audit package and review its completeness — this prevents surprise gaps from accumulating.
Knowledge Check
What distinguishes an "audit-ready" organization from one that is not?
An audit-ready organization has passed at least one external audit in the past 24 months and holds a current compliance certification.
An audit-ready organization can generate a complete, accurate audit package with fresh evidence and documented exceptions at any point in the operating year — not only in response to an announced assessment.
An audit-ready organization has automated evidence collection for all UCB controls, eliminating the need for manual attestations.
An audit-ready organization has zero open exceptions at the time of assessment.
Move on when you've generated an audit package and produced an executive assurance brief.
