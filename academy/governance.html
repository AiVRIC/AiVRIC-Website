Governance Operating Model

The organizational framework that makes governance continuous rather than periodic.

What the governance operating model is

A governance operating model is the organizational structure that links your policy commitments to the operational controls that fulfill them, the evidence that proves those controls work, and the risk accountability that owns the gaps. Without a model, governance becomes reactive — audits happen, findings emerge, and remediation is disconnected from policy intent.

In CloudSignals, the governance operating model has five components that form a chain: Policies define what the organization commits to. Controls describe how those commitments are implemented. Risks represent what could prevent fulfillment. Evidence proves controls are operating. Accountability assigns ownership of each element to a named individual or team.

ComponentWhat it answersWhere in CloudSignals
PoliciesWhat are we committed to?Governance & Assurance / UCB Policy Library
ControlsHow are we implementing commitments?UCB Control Catalog, Framework Explorer
RisksWhat could prevent fulfillment?RiskOps / Risk Register
EvidenceHow do we know controls work?Evidence Fabric, Assessment Objectives
AccountabilityWho owns each element?Asset owners, managed entities, control owners

Why this matters operationally

Governance programs fail at two predictable points. First: controls are documented but not verified — the policy exists, the procedure exists, but no evidence confirms the control actually runs. Second: accountability is diffuse — everyone is responsible for a control, which means no one is. The governance operating model forces both problems into the open by requiring evidence and named accountability for every control.

A policy defines what the organization commits to; a control describes how that commitment is implemented and verified.

A policy is evidence that a control exists; a control is the risk register entry tied to that evidence.

Unified Control Baseline

The vendor-neutral control catalog that maps your posture to any framework.

What the UCB is

The Unified Control Baseline (UCB) is AiVRIC's vendor-neutral control catalog — a structured set of security and privacy controls organized by domain and principle, each mapped to authoritative sources including ISO 27001, SOC 2, PCI DSS, NIST CSF, NIST 800-53, HIPAA Security, and others. The UCB is the source of truth for what "good" looks like in your environment, independent of which compliance framework you're reporting against.

This means you define controls once in the UCB and map them to multiple frameworks simultaneously. When an assessor asks for your ISO 27001 control evidence, and separately your SOC 2 CC controls, you draw from the same evidence set — you don't maintain parallel programs.

UCB structure: domains, principles, and controls

UCB controls are organized in a three-level hierarchy. Domains are the major security areas (e.g., Identity & Access Management, Data Protection, Infrastructure Security, Incident Response). Principles are the governing intent within a domain (e.g., "Authenticate all users before granting access"). Controls are the specific, testable requirements that implement each principle (e.g., "MFA is enforced for all privileged accounts").

In the platform — Explore UCB and generate an Internal Baseline
1
Navigate to Governance & Assurance > Unified Control Baseline. You'll see the full domain list. Each domain shows your current coverage percentage.
2
Click into Identity & Access Management. Review the principles under that domain and the controls beneath each principle. Note which controls have evidence linked versus which are unverified.
3
Open the Framework Explorer. Select ISO 27001 and review which UCB controls map to each Annex A control. This is how you translate posture data into framework-language for auditors.
4
To generate an Internal Baseline, go to Reports > Governance Baseline and select your current UCB snapshot. This creates a timestamped record of your control state — useful as a baseline for the next assessment period.
5
Note any domains with coverage below 50%. These represent areas where the platform has controls defined but evidence has not been linked — your highest-priority governance gap areas.
Framework scores are not the same as governance compliance. The Framework Explorer shows how your scanner metadata maps to framework controls — but scanner metadata alone cannot demonstrate compliance. Evidence of implementation and effectiveness is what makes a control "compliant." The UCB requires both.
Identify your lowest-coverage UCB domains: List the three domains with the fewest linked evidence artifacts.
Generate an Internal Baseline: Create a timestamped baseline from your current UCB state. This becomes your governance starting point.
Map to your primary framework: In the Framework Explorer, identify which framework is most relevant to your compliance obligations and review your mapped posture.
Knowledge Check
What is the primary advantage of the UCB over maintaining separate programs for each compliance framework?
The UCB automatically generates compliance reports for any framework without requiring manual evidence collection.
Controls are defined once in the UCB and map to multiple frameworks simultaneously — eliminating duplicate evidence programs for ISO 27001, SOC 2, PCI DSS, and others.
The UCB replaces the need for external compliance frameworks by providing a single AiVRIC-certified standard.
UCB scores are directly accepted by all major certifying bodies as audit evidence without additional documentation.

Privacy Governance

Extend the governance model to cover personal data obligations across ISO 27701, GDPR, CCPA/CPRA, and HIPAA.

Privacy as a governance domain

Privacy governance applies the same operating model as security governance — policies, controls, evidence, accountability — but specifically to personal data handling obligations. The key difference is that privacy obligations are externally defined by regulation (GDPR, CCPA/CPRA, HIPAA) and standards (ISO 27701), and the consequences of non-compliance include regulatory fines, litigation, and reputational damage that directly affect business continuity.

In CloudSignals, the Privacy Explorer surfaces applicable privacy controls from UCB, grouped by regulation and data type, and shows your readiness state — not just whether controls are documented, but whether evidence confirms they operate as required.

Privacy readiness vs. privacy compliance

Privacy readiness measures whether your organization can demonstrate evidence of controls for applicable privacy obligations — not just whether controls are documented. A policy that says "we encrypt PHI in transit" is not readiness; a configuration export and a scan result confirming encryption is enabled on every PHI-handling endpoint is readiness. The Privacy Explorer scores readiness on this basis.

Evidence Governance

The provenance, freshness, and trust chain that makes evidence audit-ready.

What evidence governance is

Evidence governance is the discipline of managing proof that controls operate as designed. It is not enough to collect evidence once — evidence has a lifecycle. It was collected at a point in time, from a specific source, by a specific method. It can become stale. It can be revoked. It can conflict with other evidence. Without governance, an evidence collection becomes a pile of files with unclear provenance and unknown freshness.

The Evidence Fabric in CloudSignals gives every evidence artifact four properties: provenance (where it came from and how it was collected), freshness (how recent it is relative to the control's assessment cycle), trust level (automated/scanner data vs. human-reviewed attestation), and reviewer linkage (who validated it and when).

Evidence freshness and assurance drift

Every UCB control has an assessment cadence — quarterly, annually, or per-change. When evidence exceeds that window, its freshness degrades. The platform marks it as stale and reduces the control's confidence score. When enough controls degrade, overall assurance drifts below your target confidence level — a state called assurance drift. The Assurance Calendar surfaces controls approaching or past their review window so you can proactively collect new evidence before drift occurs.

The reviewer workflow is your audit trail. When a human reviewer validates evidence and attests to its accuracy, that attestation — with timestamp and reviewer identity — becomes part of the evidence chain. This is what distinguishes governance-grade evidence from a raw configuration export.
In the platform — Link evidence to a control and review readiness
1
Navigate to Governance & Assurance > Evidence Fabric. Review the evidence inventory — note the freshness distribution (fresh, aging, stale) across your controls.
2
Select a control with stale evidence. Click Add Evidence and upload or link the appropriate artifact (configuration export, scan result, screenshot, attestation, policy document).
3
Set the evidence type (automated/scanner vs. manual/attestation) and assign a reviewer. The reviewer receives a notification to validate the artifact before it counts toward confidence score.
4
After the reviewer validates, note the control's confidence score update. Compare the "before" and "after" confidence to understand how a single evidence artifact moves the dial.
5
Open the Assurance Calendar. Review which controls have upcoming review windows in the next 30 days. Schedule collection tasks for any controls within 2 weeks of their review deadline.
Audit stale evidence: Identify all controls with stale or aging evidence and create collection tasks for each.
Link at least one piece of new evidence: Upload or link an evidence artifact to a control, assign a reviewer, and observe the confidence score change.
Review the Assurance Calendar: Identify controls with review windows in the next 30 days and schedule collection tasks.
Knowledge Check
What does "assurance drift" mean in the context of evidence governance?
A control has been flagged as an exception and its evidence requirements have been formally waived.
The organization's risk appetite has changed, making previously acceptable evidence insufficient.
Enough controls have stale or missing evidence that overall assurance confidence has degraded below the target threshold — a state that requires proactive evidence collection to correct.
A compliance framework has been updated and existing evidence no longer maps correctly to the new control requirements.

Risk-to-Control Traceability

Follow the chain from control failure through residual risk to exception governance.

The traceability chain

Risk-to-control traceability means every risk in your register can be traced to a specific control failure, and every control failure can be traced forward to a governance decision. Without this chain, you can have a risk register that grows indefinitely — risks accumulate but never close because no one can confirm whether the underlying control failure has been addressed.

In CloudSignals, the chain works as follows: a scanner evaluates a resource against a UCB control and creates a finding when the control fails. The finding represents a gap between your policy commitment and your actual state. That gap — if not remediated — becomes residual risk: the risk that remains after your current controls are applied. RiskOps treatment decisions (remediate, mitigate, accept) determine how that residual risk is governed.

Exceptions: when residual risk is formally accepted

An exception is a formal acknowledgment that a specific control will not meet its requirement within a defined period, and that the associated residual risk is within policy-acceptable bounds. Exceptions require: a named owner, a business justification, a risk statement, a compensating control (if any), and an expiry date. They are not permanent — every exception must be reviewed at or before its expiry date.

Exceptions that are renewed repeatedly without a remediation plan represent a governance failure. The platform tracks exception age and renewal history, which auditors will scrutinize. A risk accepted three years running with no remediation progress is not a risk management decision — it's an unaddressed liability.

In the platform — Trace a finding to its residual risk and exception state
1
Navigate to Findings and filter by a specific UCB domain (e.g., Identity & Access Management). Select a High or Critical finding.
2
In the finding detail, click View linked control to open the UCB control that failed. Note the control's current confidence score — the finding is directly contributing to this degradation.
3
Return to the finding. Click View linked risk (if a risk register entry exists) or Create risk to promote the finding to the risk register with a residual risk assessment.
4
Navigate to Projects > Exceptions. Review existing security exceptions — note their age, expiry dates, and whether a compensating control is documented. Any exceptions approaching expiry need owner review.
Trace a finding to its UCB control: Select a High finding and navigate to its parent control. Document the relationship between the finding severity and control confidence.
Review exceptions for expiry: Audit exceptions expiring within 60 days. For each, confirm the owner is aware and a renewal or remediation decision is pending.
Identify repeatedly-renewed exceptions: Flag any exceptions in their third renewal cycle or beyond. These require escalation — repeated acceptance without remediation progress is not defensible governance.
Knowledge Check
What must be true for a security exception to be considered valid governance evidence?
It must be approved by the CISO and reviewed annually.
It must have a named owner, a business justification, a risk statement, an expiry date, and — where applicable — a documented compensating control.
It must be linked to a finding that has been verified as low severity.
It must be renewed every 90 days regardless of the residual risk level.
Move on when you've traced at least one finding to its UCB control and reviewed your exceptions for upcoming expiry.

AI-Assisted Governance

Use Vision and GRC AI as governance accelerators — with mandatory human validation.

Two AI systems, two roles

CloudSignals provides two distinct AI capabilities for governance work. Vision AI is a security analysis engine that examines findings, asset states, and posture signals to surface patterns, prioritize risks, and generate investigation narratives. GRC AI is a governance-specific assistant that helps draft control assessments, identify evidence gaps, and generate assurance briefs from your UCB state.

Both are advisory tools — they accelerate governance work, they do not replace human judgment. Every AI output that becomes part of your governance record must include: the assumptions the model used, a confidence indicator, and a named human reviewer who attested to the output before it was accepted.

The human validation requirement

The reason AI outputs require human validation in governance contexts is accountability. If a control is assessed as "effective" based on an AI-generated analysis that turns out to be incorrect, the accountability question becomes: who reviewed and accepted that output? Without a named validator, there is no accountable party — and no defensible governance record. The platform enforces this by requiring a reviewer attestation before an AI-generated assessment can be promoted to evidence status.

AI-generated answers are advisory only. When using GRC AI or Vision to analyze a control gap or generate a risk narrative, treat the output as a first draft requiring your expert review — not a final answer. Check the model's stated assumptions, verify cited findings exist and are current, and attest to accuracy before committing the output to your governance record.
In the platform — Generate and validate an AI-assisted assurance brief
1
Navigate to Vision in the main navigation. Click Ask Vision and enter a governance question — for example: "What are the top three control gaps in our Identity & Access Management domain?"
2
Review Vision's response. Note the assumptions panel — which findings, assets, and evidence artifacts was the analysis based on? Verify that the cited artifacts are current and accurately described.
3
Navigate to Governance & Assurance > GRC AI. Request an Executive Assurance Brief for a specific UCB domain. Review the drafted brief for accuracy.
4
Edit the brief where needed — correct any inaccuracies, add context the model didn't have, and fill in the Reviewer attestation field before saving. This attestation is your evidence that the output was human-validated.
5
Export the validated brief. This is now a governance artifact — suitable for sharing with leadership, an auditor, or the Authorizing Official as an assurance summary.
Generate a Vision analysis: Ask Vision a governance question and review the assumptions panel to assess output reliability.
Draft and validate an assurance brief: Use GRC AI to generate a brief, review it for accuracy, and add your reviewer attestation before saving.
Document your AI governance rule: Write a one-paragraph team policy on when AI outputs require review before use as evidence — share it with your GRC team.
Knowledge Check
What makes an AI-generated governance output acceptable as audit evidence in CloudSignals?
It was generated by a certified AI model version and has a confidence score above 85%.
The AI output was reviewed by at least two team members and stored in the evidence repository.
A named human reviewer has attested to the output's accuracy, assumptions have been reviewed, and the attestation is recorded as part of the evidence artifact.
The output was generated from scanner data rather than manual input, making it inherently more reliable for audit purposes.
Move on when you've generated and validated at least one AI-assisted governance output.
